Paul Merolla

I build more intelligent computers, drawing on neuroscience, chip design, and machine learning.

I co-founded MK1 in 2023 focusing on AI inference. Our products grew to serve over a trillion tokens a day, and in 2025 MK1 was acquired by AMD, where our team now works in the Artificial Intelligence Group.

Before that, I was on the founding team at Neuralink, where I co-led chip design and developed the algorithms that decode brain activity. Earlier in my career, I designed brain-inspired chips at IBM and Stanford.

News

November 10, 2025

MK1 joins AMD

When we started MK1 in early 2023, ChatGPT had just launched and there was an obvious gap between what AI could theoretically do and what it could reliably deliver in the real world. Our vision was simple: build foundational technology to make AI fast, efficient, and generally useful.

We delivered. Our Flywheel inference engine now serves over a trillion tokens a day, and our Comprehension Engine gives LLMs a scalable reasoning and memory layer for coding applications and complex enterprise workflows.

I'm excited that MK1 is joining AMD, in Anush Elangovan's organization. Joining AMD is the right next step for our technology and our mission: we'll have the resources and platform to reach the scale we always envisioned, while our team continues building what we started. I'm deeply grateful to our backers, our customers, and especially the MK1 team, whose talent and execution turned our vision into reality.

June 3, 2022

Off to a new adventure

After five and a half years, I decided to leave Neuralink to pursue another project. It was a difficult decision: I still find Neuralink's mission extremely compelling, and I was having a lot of fun developing algorithms to decode brain activity. What I'll miss most is working with the incredibly talented and passionate team. I look forward to seeing our work improve the lives of patients who suffer from brain injury.

2016

Convolutional networks for energy-efficient neuromorphic computing

Our PNAS paper asks how deep learning can work within the constraints of low-power neural hardware. We describe a modified backpropagation that reaches near state-of-the-art accuracy with discrete synapses, limited-precision communication, and restricted connectivity, running live on TrueNorth across eight datasets at 1,200–2,600 frames per second while using only 25–275 mW.

August 8, 2014

TrueNorth on the cover of Science

TrueNorth's architecture is inspired by the structure and function of the brain: a 64×64 array of neurosynaptic cores, each combining neurons (computation), synapses (memory), and a spike router (communication). With 5.4 billion transistors, it has one million neurons and 256 million synapses, runs in real time, and consumes less than 100 mW. I was one of the lead chip architects.

Read the Science article or the pre-print (PDF).

April 26, 2014

Neurogrid in Proceedings of the IEEE

Neurogrid is an analog neuromorphic supercomputer made of sixteen Neurocore chips (pictured) connected in a tree. I co-led the design with John Arthur, Rodrigo Alvarez, and Kwabena Boahen.
